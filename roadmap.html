Product roadmap · working draft · Nov 2026

Scan a product. See what it's really made of.

A phone app that scans a barcode or a label, or reads a shared product link, and explains materials, origin, ingredients and safety in plain language, with a source behind every flag. It works in the store and before an online purchase. It starts with clothing and accessories, then adds cosmetics, supplements, home goods and furniture.

Ground rules

What keeps the app trustworthy

The app makes statements about real brands. These rules apply to every feature below and are the reason people will believe it.

  • Every flag cites a source and a dateNo flag ships without a link to the record, label or rule it comes from.
  • Report gaps, not accusations"Fabric origin not disclosed" is a fact. "Secretly made in China" is a lawsuit.
  • Brands can never pay to remove a flagThey can respond and upload proof. Money never changes a grade.
  • Public methodology and corrections logAnyone can see how grades are calculated and what we fixed when we got it wrong.
  • No medical adviceHealth and interaction flags point people to a pharmacist or doctor.
  • Health details stay privateAllergies, pregnancy and skin conditions stay on the phone where possible, are used only with consent, and are never sold.
  • No hidden affiliate linksIf alternatives are ever suggested, any commercial link is clearly labeled.
Core loop

How a scan works

Each product only needs to be read once. After that, everyone who scans the same barcode gets the answer instantly.

  1. 1Scan or shareScan a barcode in a store, or share a product link. Known product: report right away.
  2. 2Read the sourceNew product: photo of the label or stamp, or the listing's text and spec photos.
  3. 3AI extracts itFibers, ingredients, origin, certifications and claims, as structured data.
  4. 4Run the checksWording, contradictions, recalls, chemicals, registry lookups.
  5. 5Save and explainReport in plain language, stored under the barcode for the next person.
Shared vocabulary

How results are worded

Claim verdicts

  • SupportedLabel, registry or evidence backs the claim.
  • ContradictedA source in hand says otherwise, such as the spec table or a certificate.
  • Misleading wordingTechnically legal but implies more, like "genuine leather" or "gold tone."
  • Can't verifyNeeds a lab test. We say so instead of guessing.

Origin transparency grade

  • Fully disclosedEvery production stage named, ideally with a checkable certificate.
  • Partly disclosedAssembly plus fabric or material origin known.
  • Final step onlyJust a "Made in X" label.
  • ContradictedLabel disagrees with the brand's own supplier list or certificate.

Example: a jacket labeled "Made in Italy"

  • Raw materialnot disclosed
  • Fabric: woven and dyednot disclosed
  • Components: zipper, buttons, liningnot disclosed
  • Cutting and sewingItaly · the only stage the label covers
  • Finishingnot disclosed

In the EU, "Made in X" names the country of the last major processing step. Italy's stricter "100% Made in Italy" claim requires design, materials and manufacturing all in Italy.

Phase 1
Clothing · shoes · bags · jewelry

Launch: read the label or the listing, explain it

Works in a store and before an online purchase, where misleading claims do the most damage because you can't see the label yet. Built on label photos, product listings and a seeded database, so it's useful from day one.

Done when (example targets): 1,000 weekly users, 6 in 10 scans end in a useful report, and fewer than 1 in 100 flags overturned after review.

Core

  • Barcode scan

    Scan any UPC or EAN. If the product is already known, the report appears instantly.

    Your own shared database
  • Label reader Key

    Photo of the care label, stamp or tag. AI pulls out fiber percentages, country of origin, care instructions and any claims.

    Claude Haiku 5.5 vision, about 0.3¢ per new product (estimate)
  • Listing reader Key

    Share a product from Amazon, Shein, Temu, Zara or any brand site, or paste the link. AI reads the listing text and spec photos so claims are checked before you buy.

    The listing page, Claude Haiku 5.5
  • Materials explained

    "81% nylon, 19% elastane: synthetic, stretchy, durable, less breathable than cotton." A simple view by default, with an expert view for the chemistry.

    Built-in knowledge packs per category
  • Personal rules Key

    "No polyester," "natural fibers only," "vegan," "nickel allergy," "made in EU." Every scan shows green or red against your own rules.

    Stored on the phone
  • Seeded database

    Before launch, read the product pages of the top few hundred items from H&M, Zara, Lululemon, Shein and similar brands, which list composition, so early scans already hit known products.

    Brand product pages (check each site's terms first)

Checks

  • Wording decoder Key

    Translates sales terms into what they legally mean: gold plated or gold tone, genuine, bonded or PU leather, "cashmere feel," satin, bamboo that is really rayon, "Designed in Italy."

    FTC Jewelry Guides, Textile and Wool Acts, EU textile labeling rules
  • Listing vs label contradictions Key

    The listing says "100% cotton," the label or spec table says polyester. The same check covers metal, leather and origin.

    The listing and the label
  • Origin breakdown

    Shows which production stages the "Made in" label covers and which are unknown, then assigns the transparency grade.

    Label wording, brand disclosures

Trust and privacy ship before launch

  • Methodology page

    Exactly how each verdict and grade is calculated, in plain language.

    Public page
  • Corrections log

    Every fix to a published result, with date and reason.

    Public page
  • Health profile privacy Key

    Allergies, pregnancy and skin conditions are health data. Keep them on the phone where possible, ask for explicit consent, and never sell or share them. User data never goes into the business API.

    GDPR special category data, US state health-data laws such as Washington's My Health My Data Act
  • Photo handling

    Label photos are cropped to the label. Faces, receipts and anything else in frame are discarded, not stored.

    App policy
Phase 1.5
Same categories

Launch +1: safety and authenticity

Added once the core loop works. Each check here has known limits, so results are worded as possible matches or general information, never as findings about a specific product.

Done when: recall and counterfeit results have been reviewed for false alarms on 200 real scans before they're switched on for everyone.

Checks

  • Recall alerts

    Matched by brand, product name and model number. Recall records rarely include barcodes, so results show as a possible match unless the model number matches exactly.

    US CPSC recalls database, EU Safety Gate
  • Counterfeit check

    Compares the company that registered the barcode with the brand on the tag. It only catches lazy fakes: most counterfeits copy the real barcode, so a match proves nothing. Resellers and store labels also cause mismatches, so a mismatch is shown as worth a closer look, never as proof.

    GS1 barcode registry (lookups are rate-limited)
  • Chemical information

    General information for the category unless test data exists for that exact product: "Water-repellent finishes often use PFAS. This brand says PFAS-free: yes / not stated." Covers PFAS, BPA in polyester-spandex, lead and cadmium in cheap jewelry, nickel, and formaldehyde finishes.

    State PFAS rules, published test results, brand statements
  • Gold value floor

    For "solid gold" claims: weight × purity × today's gold price. A 10 g "solid 14K" chain priced at $39 can't be solid gold.

    Live gold spot price
Phase 2
Cosmetics · supplements · food · community

Next: new categories and the community

Cosmetics and food come first because open databases already cover millions of barcodes. Community features arrive once there are enough users to fill them.

Done when (example targets): cosmetics and supplement scans reach the same useful-report rate as clothing, and the first 20 brands have claimed their pages.

Cosmetics

  • Ingredient decoder Key

    Explains each ingredient on the list and flags ones restricted in the EU but allowed in the US.

    Open Beauty Facts (share-alike license, kept separate), EU cosmetics ingredient database (CosIng)
  • Unregulated buzzwords

    "Clean," "non-toxic," "hypoallergenic," "dermatologist tested" have no legal definition in the US. Shown as marketing.

    Knowledge pack
  • Fragrance and allergens

    "Parfum" can hide many chemicals. Flags declared allergens for sensitive skin.

    EU fragrance allergen labeling list
  • Skin profile

    Acne-prone, pregnant, sensitive, fragrance-free. Personal rules applied to ingredients.

    Stored per user
  • Cruelty-free check

    Looks the brand up instead of trusting a bunny logo the brand drew itself.

    Leaping Bunny brand database
  • Reef-safe and expiry

    Checks "reef-safe" against banned sunscreen ingredients. Decodes the open-jar symbol and batch code.

    Hawaii sunscreen ingredient ban, label symbols
  • Label translation

    Korean, Japanese and Chinese labels translated and explained.

    Claude vision

Supplements

  • Dose reality check Key

    "Contains 50 mg of ashwagandha. Studies used 300 to 600 mg." Every dose comes from an evidence table reviewed by someone qualified, never from the AI's memory.

    NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, published studies, reviewed in-house
  • Tainted product list Key

    Flags products the FDA found with hidden drugs, most often in weight-loss and "male enhancement" supplements.

    FDA tainted supplements database
  • Proprietary blends

    Flags "Energy Blend 800 mg" style labels that hide how much of each ingredient is inside.

    Supplement Facts panel
  • Third-party testing

    Verifies NSF Certified for Sport and USP Verified claims against the certifiers' lists.

    NSF and USP public databases
  • Upper limits and athlete mode

    Warns when a dose passes the safe upper limit (vitamin A, iron, B6). Optional banned-in-sport flags.

    NIH upper intake levels, anti-doping lists
  • Interaction flags

    "St. John's wort can interact with birth control. Ask a pharmacist." A flag only, never advice.

    NIH fact sheets

Food low effort to add

  • Food scan

    Ingredients, additives and nutrition from an existing open database, with the same claim checks. Kept in a separate store from your own data because of its share-alike license.

    Open Food Facts (ODbL license)

Community

  • Reviews per barcode Key

    Focused on what product pages never say: "pilled after 3 washes," "peeled in 6 months," "shrank a size." Photos after months of wear. Moderated, with a takedown and dispute process.

    Users
  • Report a misleading claim

    Reports add up per product, with a one-tap path to file with the FTC or an EU consumer authority.

    Users
  • Shrinkflation tracker

    "Was 200 ml, now 170 ml, same price." Built automatically from repeat scans of the same barcode.

    Scan history

Brand responses

  • Claim your brand page Key

    Brands reply next to a flag and upload proof such as supplier lists or certificates. A person checks every document and confirms certificates with the issuer, because fake certificates exist. Verified proof earns a badge and can raise the transparency grade.

    Brand uploads, checked by us
  • "Brand fixed this" history

    When a brand corrects a listing or label, the change is shown publicly with its date.

    Change log

Across every category

  • Regulator and lawsuit history Key

    "This brand received an FDA warning letter in 2025 for unproven claims," with the record linked.

    FDA warning letters, FTC cases, EU Safety Gate, class action trackers
  • Greenwashing detector

    Flags vague claims like "eco," "conscious" or "planet-friendly" with nothing behind them. EU rules against unproven generic environmental claims have applied since 27 September 2026.

    EU consumer rules, FTC Green Guides
  • Side-by-side compare

    Scan two products in the store and see them next to each other.

    Scan results
  • Cost per use

    Cost per wear for clothes, per effective dose for supplements, per ml for cosmetics.

    Price entered or scanned
Phase 3
Home goods · furniture · ethics · more

Later: home goods, bigger items and supply chains

Home goods come first because their health claims are certified to numbered standards, model by model, in public registries, so most claims can be checked rather than marked "can't verify." Furniture is often bought online and has fewer barcodes in stores, so it relies more on label photos and listing links.

Done when: each new category meets the launch quality bar before the next one starts.

Home goods with health claims water filters · air purifiers · kitchenware · cleaners

  • Model number first Key

    Certifications are listed per model, not per brand or barcode, so the label and listing readers pull out the model number, certified standards, each contaminant with its reduction percentage, and rated capacity.

    Product box, Performance Data Sheet, listing specs
  • Filter certification Key

    Checks each claim against the model's listing. "Reduces lead" needs NSF/ANSI 53, PFOA/PFOS needs 473, and drug residues need 401; 42 covers only taste and odor. Catches the same brand selling filters with different certifications, like a basic pitcher filter that isn't certified for lead while the brand's premium filter is.

    NSF, WQA Gold Seal and IAPMO certification listings (check terms before bulk lookups)
  • Performance Data Sheet reader

    Reads the table of contaminants, reduction percentages and gallons per filter that certified models must provide. Gives cost per gallon and filter-change reminders.

    Manufacturer data sheet, Claude vision
  • Wording decoder for home goods

    "Tested to NSF standards" and "meets NSF 53" are not certification. "Removes 99% of contaminants" doesn't say which ones. "HEPA-type" is not true HEPA. "PFOA-free" cookware means little now that PFOA has been phased out in the US; "PTFE-free" is the claim that matters. "Alkaline," "structured" and "non-toxic" have no standard behind them.

    Certifier mark-use rules, US DOE HEPA definition, EPA PFOA stewardship program
  • Fake replacement filters

    Fridge and pitcher filters are commonly counterfeited. The same barcode-owner check from Phase 1.5, plus a check that the certification listing names this exact model and seller's part number.

    GS1 barcode registry, certification listings
  • Air purifier checks

    Verifies the clean-air delivery rate (CADR) and room size claims. Flags purifiers missing from California's list of models certified for low ozone output.

    AHAM Verifide directory, California Air Resources Board air cleaner list
  • Kitchenware and containers

    Nonstick coatings, "BPA-free" claims, and lead in imported aluminum cookware and drinkware parts. Shown as general information unless test data exists for that product.

    FDA import alerts and warnings, published test results, brand statements
  • Antibacterial and cleaner claims

    Products that claim to kill germs, like silver-treated filters, generally need EPA registration; a missing EPA number is a flag. Cleaners can be checked against the Safer Choice list.

    EPA pesticide registration rules (FIFRA), EPA Safer Choice database

Furniture

  • Real material Key

    Solid wood vs veneer vs particleboard. "Genuine" vs bonded leather on sofas.

    Knowledge pack, listing specs
  • Formaldehyde label

    Composite wood sold in the US must carry a compliance label. Missing label is a flag.

    EPA TSCA Title VI rule
  • Flame retardants

    California-compliant upholstery is labeled with whether flame retardants were added. The app reads that label.

    California TB 117-2013 label
  • Tip-over safety

    Checks dressers against the US anti-tip standard. Important for parents.

    CPSC clothing storage unit standard
  • Fabric durability

    Explains "double rubs": 15,000 is light use, 50,000 and up is heavy duty.

    Wyzenbeek and Martindale test ratings
  • Certification lookups

    Verifies wood sourcing and low-emission claims.

    FSC and GREENGUARD databases

Risks

Risks to manage

  • Open-database licenses. Open Food Facts and Open Beauty Facts use ODbL, a share-alike license. Merging their data into your own published database could require releasing it under the same license. Keep their data separate, and check certifier and retailer terms before any bulk use.
  • AI getting facts wrong. Extracted claims are checked against the source text. Anything stated as evidence, such as doses or legal definitions, comes from reviewed tables, not the AI's memory.
  • Brand verification workload. Someone has to check documents and confirm certificates with issuers. Budget for it before opening brand pages.
  • User reviews. Needs moderation and a takedown and dispute process, separate from the app's own flags.
  • False alarms. Counterfeit and recall matches stay "possible" until confirmed. Test them on real scans before turning them on.
Decisions

Not doing

  • Reverse image search. Dropped. Paid per search and not needed now that labels and barcodes are the input.
  • Per-item carbon footprint numbers. The data is too weak; the app would be greenwashing itself.
  • Paid flag removal or paid better scores. The day this exists, the app is worthless.
  • Licensing other companies' ratings. Costly and creates dependence. Build grades from public sources instead.
  • Unlabeled affiliate links. Undercuts the trust everything else depends on.
